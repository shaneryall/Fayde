    Fayde plate solver: literature review¶

    Companion to docs/design/plate-solver.md (the "design doc"; section numbers like §5.3 refer to it, and "entry 3.1" to this survey's own entries). Fayde's design: pair seeds when a pointing and a tight scale are given, a 5-ratio pattern hash otherwise, across indexes for different field widths, astrometry.net-style log-odds verification, a weighted TAN+SIP fit, and UCAC4 or Tycho-2.

    Scope and method. Web search, then reading of papers, abstracts and project pages. Each entry says how much was read:

    • [full]: the full text was read, or the relevant sections of it.
    • [abstract]: the abstract or publisher summary only.
    • [secondary]: known only through citing papers, surveys or indexes.

    Most IEEE/AIAA papers are paywalled and were assessed at abstract level. Verdicts assume the regime Fayde is built for: about 1° FOV, about 3″/px, 30–60 sources, many false detections, stars often trailed, and usually a pointing prior.

    Already covered by the design doc and not repeated here: Lang et al. 2010 (astrometry.net: geometric hashing with quads plus Bayesian verification), tetra3 (ESA's rewrite of Tetra, Brown & Stubis 2017: 4-star edge-ratio hash), cedar-solve (a uniform-database fork of tetra3).

    1. Star identification / lost-in-space (star-tracker literature)¶

    Context that drives every verdict below. Star-tracker algorithms are tuned for wide FOVs (8–20°), bright stars, tiny onboard catalogues and no prior. Fayde's hinted path is different: it has a pointing prior, an often tight scale prior, sometimes a roll prior, and a narrow field crowded with false detections. What carries over is mostly about robustness to false stars, and the main lesson is simple: the fewer true stars a hypothesis needs, the more robust the matcher.

    1.1 Pyramid and the k-vector¶

    • Citations. Mortari, Samaan, Bruccoleri & Junkins, "The Pyramid star identification technique", Navigation 51(3), 2004. Mortari, "Search-less algorithm for star pattern recognition", J. Astronautical Sci. 45(2), 1997 (Springer). Mortari & Neta, "k-vector range searching techniques", AAS 00-128, 2000. [secondary] (described consistently in Leake et al. 2020 and in surveys; originals not read.)
    • Core idea. The k-vector is a precomputed index over the sorted angular distances of all catalogue star pairs. A range query ("every pair with separation in [d−ε, d+ε]") then costs O(1) plus the number of results, with no comparisons. Pyramid takes image star triples, looks each edge up in the k-vector, intersects the candidate sets to identify a triangle, then confirms it with a fourth "pyramid" star. A triangle that the fourth star cannot confirm is rejected, which is how false stars get discarded.
    • Evidence. Widely called the nominal lost-in-space algorithm. In a six-algorithm comparison (cited in the search results; not read in full) Pyramid had the highest average accuracy with false stars and was the fastest. Its known weakness is a need for accurate focal length (Leake et al. 2020). Fayde's scale prior covers that. The winner of the ESA Kelvins star-ID challenge ("Super k-ID") was built on k-vector techniques (results, [full]).
    • Verdict: ADOPT the idea, not the algorithm (§5.3). In hinted mode, build a sorted pair list (a k-vector) of the catalogue stars inside the hint cone, at query time. That is a few hundred stars and about 10⁵ pairs, built in well under a millisecond. Then seed hypotheses from image star pairs. With a scale prior and known parity, two stars fix a full similarity transform. See entry 3.1 for why 2-star seeds beat 4-star patterns when many sources are false. Fayde tries the pair seeds before its pattern hash (§5.2a).

    1.2 Grid algorithm, and its Bayesian and polar variants¶

    • Citations. Padgett & Kreutz-Delgado, "A grid algorithm for autonomous star identification", IEEE TAES 33(1), 1997. Clouse & Padgett, "Small field-of-view star identification using Bayesian decision theory", IEEE TAES 36(3), 773–783, 2000 (doi:10.1109/7.869495). Lee et al. 2007 (polar grid). [abstract]
    • Core idea. Rotate the field so that a pivot star's nearest neighbour lies on the x-axis, rasterize the neighbours onto a coarse grid, and compare bit patterns. Clouse & Padgett add a Bayesian, adaptive acceptance threshold aimed at a 2° FOV.
    • Evidence. Clouse & Padgett report 96.0% correct with <0.3% false positives at 0.5 px noise and 0.8 mag noise. Grid methods depend on the axis-defining neighbour being real and detected, which false or missing stars break. Houtz & Frueh (entry 2.1) say this explicitly for streaked images.
    • Verdict: SKIP as a matcher. CONSIDER Clouse & Padgett only as further support for likelihood-ratio acceptance with magnitude information (entry 3.7), which Fayde does not use (§5.4).

    1.3 Geometric voting¶

    • Citation. Kolomenkin, Pollak, Shimshoni & Lindenbaum, "Geometric voting algorithm for star trackers", IEEE TAES 44(2), 441–456, 2008, doi:10.1109/TAES.2008.4560198. [abstract]
    • Core idea. Every catalogue pair whose angular distance matches an image pair votes for both identities. Each image star takes the identity with the most votes, and a second voting round checks the result. The method uses geometry only and draws on the whole image rather than a chosen subset.
    • Evidence. The authors report robustness to non-star objects. The LOST open-source tracker (docs) notes "no statistical guarantees" on the wrong-answer rate.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER as a hinted fallback (§5.3). Inside a hint cone with a tight scale prior, pair voting is cheap and degrades gracefully when the brightest sources are false. Fayde has no voting stage (§5.3); its log-odds test, with the hypothesis-count correction, would gate the votes.

    1.4 Liebe triangle¶

    • Citation. Liebe, "Pattern recognition of star constellations for spacecraft applications", IEEE AES Magazine 8(1), 31–39, 1993, doi:10.1109/62.180383. [secondary]
    • Core idea. A star plus its two nearest neighbours gives a triangle feature (two distances and an angle).
    • Evidence. The ICPRAM 2023 related-work section and others: fast, but it "suffered limitations with the introduction of false stars". A single false nearest neighbour breaks it.
    • Verdict: SKIP.

    1.5 SP-Search and recursive mode¶

    • Citation. Samaan, Mortari & Junkins, "Recursive mode star identification algorithms", IEEE TAES 41(4), 2005. [secondary]
    • Core idea. Given the previous attitude, predict which catalogue stars fall in the FOV and match locally, with no pattern search.
    • Verdict: §5.6 sequence mode is a narrower form: one WCS predicted from a solved neighbour, tried last, when the search finds nothing.

    1.6 Multi-Poles Algorithm (MPA)¶

    • Citations. Schiattarella, Spiller & Curti, "A novel star identification technique robust to high presence of false objects: the Multi-Poles Algorithm", Adv. Space Res. 59(8), 2133–2147,
    • Schiattarella, Spiller & Curti, "Star identification robust to angular rates and false objects with rolling shutter compensation", Acta Astronautica, 2020 (preprint, [full] for the 2020 paper, which summarizes the 2017 one).
    • Core idea. Several "pole" stars are each tried as the centre of a pair-distance pattern, in three phases: acceptance, verification, confirmation.
    • Evidence. 100% success with up to 185 false objects against 33 catalogued stars, falling to 66% at 486 false objects against 31 stars (stable attitude). Second in the ESA Kelvins challenge. The ESA-contest version's main improvement was magnitude filtering, which made the confirmation phase unnecessary.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER, as evidence. The magnitude-filtering result is independent support for a photometric term in the verifier (entry 3.7). The algorithm itself adds nothing beyond 1.1 and 1.3.

    1.7 Non-dimensional star-ID¶

    • Citation. Leake, Arnas & Mortari, "Non-dimensional star-identification", Sensors 20(9):2697, 2020 (arXiv:2003.13736). [abstract]
    • Core idea. Use dihedral angles of spherical star triangles, which do not depend on focal length, so identification survives a miscalibrated focal length or optical axis.
    • Verdict: SKIP. Fayde estimates scale itself, and its 5-ratio edge patterns are already scale-invariant (edges over the longest).

    1.8 Reverse attitude statistics¶

    • Citation. Dong, Wang, Wang & Wang, "Reverse attitude statistics based star map identification method", arXiv:2410.23758, 2024 (submitted to IEEE Sensors J.) (arXiv). [abstract]
    • Core idea. Build candidate attitudes from many matched pairs (with spatial-hash indexing), then take the mode of the attitude distribution. Matching and attitude solving happen together. Aimed at missing stars, false stars and star trailing in near-space conditions.
    • Evidence. The authors claim a >14.3% better identification rate and >28.5% faster solving than the state of the art, with field and on-orbit tests. The baselines were not visible in the abstract.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER, as the same family as 1.3: attitude-space voting. If Fayde implements the hinted fallback, voting in the (roll, offset) space of 2-star seeds is the natural form.

    1.9 Surveys and benchmarks¶

    • Citations. Spratling & Mortari, "A survey on star identification algorithms", Algorithms 2(1), 2009. Rijlaarsdam, Yous, Byrne, Oddenino, Furano & Moloney, "A survey of lost-in-space star identification algorithms since 2009", Sensors 20(9):2579, 2020 ([abstract]; the MDPI and PMC full text was blocked to automated fetch). Toloei et al., "A comparative analysis of star identification algorithms", Astrophys. Space Sci., 2020 (Springer, [abstract]). ESA Kelvins "Star Trackers: First Contact" (2016–2017) (data, results, [full]).
    • Findings.
    • Rijlaarsdam et al. find that published comparisons "can lead to inconsistent conclusions" and give considerations for simulation-based evaluation.
    • Kelvins is the only shared public benchmark found: 10,000 scenes, 10° FOV, 1920×1440, up to 80 false artifacts per scene, centroid noise under 1 px, magnitude noise, Hipparcos.
    • Final Kelvins accuracy scores out of a perfect 9921: Super k-ID 9708, MPA 9703, "Tetra" (the original hash-table approach behind tetra3) 9672, then others from 9605 down.
    • So a hash-table matcher is within about 0.4% of the best on a false-star-heavy benchmark.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER. The Kelvins scenes are synthetic, public and false-star heavy, which suits a reproducible regression test of the pattern-hash and seed matchers. LOST (GitHub) implements Pyramid, geometric voting and tetra-style matchers, an independent baseline.

    2. Matching trailed / streaked stars¶

    Overall finding. The literature is thin. No paper found does blind or hinted plate solving of trailed stars with per-source covariance, endpoints and a time-dependent WCS together. The published SSA pipelines mostly either stack the frame so stars become static or feed streak centroids to astrometry.net. For each straight star trail, Fayde's solver receives a centre and the larger of its two position errors (streak-detection §7); its input has no trail endpoints, per-source covariance matrix or time term. The useful contributions are narrower; they are listed below.

    2.1 Houtz & Frueh: streak detection and plate solving¶

    • Citation. Houtz & Frueh, "Efficient streak detection and plate-solving techniques", Proc. 8th European Conf. on Space Debris, ESA SDO, 2021 (PDF, [full]).
    • Core idea.
    • Streaks are detected with cumulative-sum line integration.
    • For plate solving, only streaks whose length and orientation match the frame's most common values are kept as stars.
    • A polar-grid pattern is built around the brightest star, with the second-brightest streak as the axis, using spherical-triangle radii and angles. It is matched by rotational correlation against catalogue patterns, centred on Tycho-2 stars near the a-priori pointing.
    • All stars are then identified from the radius/angle residuals, and QUEST gives the attitude at mid-exposure.
    • Evidence. 15 frames from the POGS 35 cm telescope: on average 11.7 streaked stars identified per frame, with star-vector errors under 1″. A small sample with no false-solve statistics.
    • Verdict: ADOPT the idea, not the matcher. This is the closest published analogue to Fayde's case. It confirms that (a) trail length and direction consistency is the strongest star/non-star cue (Fayde's detector uses it: the census and the trail-against-point comparison set p_star, streak-detection §4.1 and §4.4), and (b) a hinted, pointing-centred search is the practical approach. One step further, not built: in target-tracking mode the common star trail is the sky's apparent motion relative to the tracked target, projected onto the detector. Given the commanded tracking rate and the exposure time, the trail's direction could constrain roll and its length the pixel scale. This is an inference of this survey, not a published method.

    2.2 Sease et al.: attitude and rate from streak endpoints¶

    • Citation. Sease, Koglin & Flewelling, "Long integration star tracker image processing for combined attitude–attitude rate estimation", Proc. SPIE 8739, 2013, doi:10.1117/12.2020004 (UCF record). [abstract], plus a catalogue-free follow-up and a UCF thesis (core.ac.uk, [secondary]).
    • Core idea. Treat streak endpoints as corner features. Group the endpoints into "start" and "end" sets using a cross-product test of the rotation sense, then run ordinary star-ID and attitude determination on each set. Two attitudes from one image give the attitude rate.
    • Evidence. Monte Carlo: endpoint accuracy better than 0.1 px when more than 20° from the rotation axis, for streaks up to 40% of the FOV. The thesis reports rates within 2% in simulation.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER. A joint fit of the mid-exposure WCS plus pointing and roll rates would be the least-squares generalization of this: one fit, with covariances, instead of two independent solves. Fayde fits a static WCS (§5.5). Note the asymmetry: endpoint error along the trail is usually much larger than across it (entry 2.4), so endpoint covariances must be anisotropic.

    2.3 AURORAS: time-dependent astrometric calibration¶

    • Citation. Krantz, Bloch, Serda, Flechtner & McMahon-Crabtree, "AURORAS: orbit determination with just one look", AMOS 2025 (PDF, [full], relevant sections).
    • Core idea. Event-camera or fast-frame data are shift-stacked so the stars become static and can be plate solved against Gaia. Then a "dynamic astrometric calibration" fits an (x, y, t) → (RA, Dec, t) transform. Residuals before the time-dependent fit show offsets and curvature from the moving tangent plane plus mount jitter; after it they are flat.
    • Evidence. Qualitative residual plots; no numbers on astrometric accuracy were given.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER a time-dependent WCS, with an optional quadratic time term (pointing acceleration) for non-linear trails, as in long exposures tracking fast LEO targets. Fayde fits a static WCS (§5.5); curved star paths are handled in detection (streak-detection §8).

    2.4 Trail fitting for astrometry (producer side)¶

    • Citation. Vereš, Jedicke, Denneau, Wainscoat, Holman & Lin, "Improved asteroid astrometry and photometry with trail fitting", PASP 124, 1197, 2012 (arXiv:1209.6106). [abstract]
    • Core idea. Model a trail as a Gaussian PSF convolved with a line segment, and fit its centre, length, angle and flux.
    • Evidence. About 3× better astrometry and up to 2 mag better photometry than PSF or aperture methods for trails longer than about 10 px (Pan-STARRS1).
    • Verdict: ADOPT the trail-model fit. Fayde's detector measures a shared trail length, angle and width, fits each trail with them held fixed, and reports errors along and across it (streak-detection §4.3). The solver takes one isotropic error per source (§6.3); errors that are narrow across the trail and wide along it, and endpoints, would need a change to the input contract and the verifier.

    2.5 Rolling-shutter streaks¶

    • Citation. Schiattarella et al. 2020 (entry 1.6), [full]. Curti et al. (rate estimation from streaks up to 10–25°/s; Sapienza, [abstract]).
    • Finding. A rolling shutter distorts streak length by about 25% (Curti et al.). A first-order compensation gives reliable identification up to 5°/s.
    • Verdict: SKIP. The frames this survey targets come from global-shutter sensors.

    2.6 Astrometry24.NET¶

    • Citation. Sybilska et al., "Astrometry24.NET – precise astrometry for SST and NEO", Proc. 1st NEO and Debris Detection Conf., ESA, 2019 (PDF, [full] for the introduction and architecture).
    • Finding. A commercial cloud service that fits streak profiles and solves frames tracked at non-sidereal rates. The paper describes the architecture and accuracy campaigns but gives little detail on the matching algorithm.
    • Verdict: SKIP. Nothing to port. It could serve as an external accuracy cross-check if real frames were ever run through it.

    3. Verification, false-match probability and robust sampling¶

    3.1 The minimal-sample argument (RANSAC)¶

    • Citation. Fischler & Bolles, "Random sample consensus", Comm. ACM 24(6), 1981. [secondary; standard result]
    • Core point. If a fraction w of sources are real catalogue stars, a hypothesis built from k sources is all-true with probability about wᵏ. At w = 0.5, a 4-star pattern (tetra3 or astrometry.net) succeeds 6.3% of the time, a 2-star seed 25%, and a 1-star seed 50%. At w = 0.3 the figures are 0.8%, 9% and 30%. For a 30–60 source xylist with many false detections, this ratio matters more than any detail of hash design.
    • Verdict: ADOPT (§5.3). When priors fix scale (and parity), the hinted path should seed from 2-star pairs (entry 1.1). Single-star seeds, when roll is also known, would be the next step: each image–catalogue pairing proposes a translation, and the translations vote (not built). The pattern hash remains the blind and weak-prior path.

    3.2 PROSAC¶

    • Citation. Chum & Matas, "Matching with PROSAC – progressive sample consensus", CVPR 2005, doi:10.1109/CVPR.2005.221 (PDF). [abstract], plus reviews.
    • Core idea. Sample hypotheses from a progressively growing set of the top-ranked correspondences rather than uniformly, which is still correct in the limit. The gain depends entirely on how well the ranking predicts correctness.
    • Evidence. Up to more than 100× fewer samples than RANSAC on CV benchmarks (in one example, 9 samples against about 10⁵); no advantage when the ordering is random.
    • Verdict: ADOPT (§5.3, §6.3). tetra3/cedar's brightest-first, breadth-first enumeration is already a PROSAC-like ordering, but it ranks by brightness alone. Fayde ranks by p_star × flux (§5.0), and its detector's p_star carries the trail consistency, which predicts "real star" much better than flux alone (the brightest sources in SSA frames are often the target, cosmic rays or blends). Fayde feeds all matchers in that order (p_star × input-order rank when there is no flux). The ordering must be deterministic (requirement 6).

    3.3 LO-RANSAC (local optimization)¶

    • Citation. Chum, Matas & Kittler, "Locally optimized RANSAC", DAGM 2003, LNCS 2781. [secondary; standard]
    • Core idea. When a hypothesis looks promising, refit the model on its inliers (and iterate) before scoring it. Minimal-sample models are noisy, so scoring the raw model underestimates support.
    • Verdict: ADOPT (§5.4/§5.5). This matters most for trailed stars and for 2-star seeds. A similarity transform from two stars, ignoring trail rate and distortion, can leave a true match just below threshold. Fayde's flow (§5.4):
    • A cheap pre-filter.
    • If it passes, a weighted refit on its matches (similarity → TAN).
    • Re-match, then compute the log-odds on the refined model.

    astrometry.net does a version of this ("tweak" during verification).

    3.4 MAGSAC / MAGSAC++¶

    • Citation. Barath, Matas & Noskova, "MAGSAC: marginalizing sample consensus", CVPR 2019; Barath, Noskova, Ivashechkin & Matas, "MAGSAC++, a fast, reliable and accurate robust estimator", CVPR 2020 (arXiv:1912.05909). [abstract]
    • Core idea. Remove the hard inlier threshold by marginalizing the model score over the noise scale σ, up to a loose upper bound, and use weighted least squares (IRLS) in place of a hard inlier set.
    • Verdict: SKIP the algorithm. Fayde has per-source position errors, so the log-odds already uses an explicit noise model, which is what MAGSAC approximates when one is missing. The underlying idea is built: producers' errors are often miscalibrated, so Fayde fits a single global error-scale factor s (covariance × s²) and reports it (§5.5), so a detector that under-reports its errors is visible. It has no prior on s and does not marginalize over it.

    3.5 A contrario / number of false alarms (NFA)¶

    • Citations. The a-contrario detection framework (Desolneux, Moisan & Morel; Abergel & Moisan on trajectories). [secondary] No paper was found that applies NFA to star identification.
    • Core idea. Accept a detection when (number of tests) × P(an event at least this good under the null hypothesis) < ε. The threshold is calibrated against the search size, not set per hypothesis.
    • Verdict: done (§5.4). astrometry.net's 10⁹ odds is a fixed per-hypothesis threshold. Blind solves test many more hypotheses than hinted ones, so the family-wise false-accept rate differs between them. Fayde counts the hypotheses tested, reports NFA = n_tested × exp(−log_odds) next to the log-odds, and in every search mode compares log_odds − ln(n_tested) with the log-odds threshold, ln(10⁹) by default. This also gives a calling pipeline a single number to record.

    3.6 Mixture-model fitting¶

    • Citation. Hogg, Bovy & Lang, "Data analysis recipes: fitting a model to data", arXiv:1008.4686, 2010. [secondary; well-known]
    • Core idea. Model each data point as a mixture: inlier (Gaussian with its covariance) or outlier (broad background), and fit or marginalize rather than hard-clip at kσ.
    • Verdict: ADOPT (§5.4/§5.5). This is the same likelihood the astrometry.net verifier uses (a Gaussian match against a uniform distractor). Fayde's final fit uses it too, as IRLS on the mixture weights with per-source outlier priors from p_star; the local refit before scoring still re-matches within shrinking radii. One noise model, used consistently, is easier to reason about and gives better-calibrated covariances.

    3.7 Photometric consistency in verification¶

    • Evidence. Clouse & Padgett (1.2) use 0.8 mag noise inside a Bayesian decision. The MPA authors (1.6) found magnitude filtering was the main improvement for their contest entry. A brightness-referenced method (Sensors 2014, [abstract]) reports speed gains from brightness ordering.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER (not built: the verifier does not compare brightnesses, §5.4). Specifics from the literature and from the regime:
    • Fit the zero point robustly.
    • Use a heavy-tailed (Student-t) magnitude residual, because UCAC4 magm against an unfiltered or wide band, trail photometry, saturation and blends all produce outliers.
    • Weight the magnitude term well below the positional term until it has been calibrated on a corpus of real frames.

    The magnitude term is a second discriminator, not a primary one.

    4. WCS fitting¶

    4.1 Wahba's problem: QUEST, ESOQ, SVD¶

    • Citations. Shuster & Oh, "Three-axis attitude determination from vector observations", J. Guid. Control 4(1), 1981 (QUEST). Mortari, "ESOQ: a closed-form solution to the Wahba problem", J. Astronaut. Sci. 45(2), 1997. Markley & Mortari, "Quaternion attitude estimation using vector observations", J. Astronaut. Sci. 48(2–3), 359, 2000, doi:10.1007/BF03546284 (preprint, under the title "New developments in quaternion estimation from vector observations": NTRS). [abstract] Umeyama, "Least-squares estimation of transformation parameters between two point patterns", IEEE TPAMI 13(4), 1991. [secondary; standard]
    • Core finding. Davenport's q-method and SVD are the most robust; QUEST, ESOQ and ESOQ2 are faster and adequate for star sensors. Robustness only matters when measurement accuracies differ widely (Markley & Mortari).
    • Verdict.
    • SKIP QUEST/ESOQ and SVD. At 30–60 stars, speed is irrelevant, and none of these solvers handles anisotropic weights anyway.
      • Fayde's pair seeds use the closed-form two-point similarity in the tangent plane (entry 3.1), once for each allowed parity; pattern seeds fit a similarity to all four stars. The one that verifies is kept. The weighted TAN least-squares fit follows, with one weight per matched pair.

    4.2 Distortion representation: SIP vs TPV vs Zernike¶

    • Citations. Shupe et al., "The SIP convention for representing distortion in FITS image headers", ASP Conf. Ser. 347, 2005. Calabretta & Greisen, "Representations of celestial coordinates in FITS", A&A 395, 2002 (TPV is a registered convention, not part of the standard). Bertin, "Automatic astrometric and photometric calibration with SCAMP", ASP Conf. Ser. 351, 2006. Shupe et al., SPIE 2012 (sip2pv/pv2sip conversion). [secondary], from survey pipeline papers (PTF, SAGES).
    • Finding. SIP (pixel-space polynomials) and TPV (polynomials in intermediate world coordinates) are interconvertible to good precision. SCAMP writes TPV, and pipelines routinely convert it to SIP because most tools read SIP. Zernike is useful only for wide-field optics with a circular pupil, and has no FITS convention.
    • Verdict: keep SIP; SKIP TPV and Zernike (§5.5, "Won't" confirmed).

    4.3 Persistent instrument distortion with per-exposure terms¶

    • Citations. Bernstein et al., "Astrometric calibration and performance of the Dark Energy Camera", PASP 129:074503, 2017 (arXiv:1703.01679, [abstract]). SCAMP (Bertin 2006), which fits distortion across many exposures at once.
    • Core idea. Split the astrometric model into a persistent instrument component (optics, detector), fitted once from many exposures, and a per-exposure component, kept low-order. DECam reaches 3–6 mas per exposure this way.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER. With 30–60 stars per frame, many of them trailed with large along-trail errors, a per-frame SIP order 3 (14 free coefficients on top of 6 linear ones) is at serious risk of overfitting. A persistent SIP fitted across solved frames could live in the instrument profile, leaving each frame only the linear terms and a low-order correction. Fayde fits SIP per frame and guards the order with held-out cells (§5.5); its profile holds no distortion.

    4.4 Model selection and covariance¶

    • Finding (standard statistics). The F-test assumes nested models with Gaussian, correctly scaled errors. With miscalibrated covariances it over-selects. BIC penalizes parameters by log N. Leave-one-out cross-validation on the matched stars is cheap at N ≤ 60 and robust to error-scale problems.
    • Verdict: ADOPT (§5.5).
    • Choose the SIP order by BIC and check it on held-out cells (Fayde's rule, §5.5).
    • Report the covariance scaled by the fitted s² (entry 3.4), with s alongside, as Fayde does.
    • Include the catalogue position error at the epoch in W (the design already does this), so the covariance is not optimistic when UCAC4 dominates the error.

    5. Catalogue and index design¶

    5.1 HEALPix uniformization¶

    • Citations. Górski et al., "HEALPix", ApJ 622, 2005. Lang et al. 2010 (uniformize-catalog).
    • Verdict: keep (§5.2). Nothing better found.

    5.2 Fibonacci lattice and spherical-spiral catalogues¶

    • Citations. González, "Measurement of areas on a sphere using Fibonacci and latitude–longitude lattices", Math. Geosci. 42, 2010 (arXiv:0912.4540, [abstract]). Bauer, "Distribution of points on a sphere with application to star catalogs", J. Guid. Control Dyn. 23(1), 2000 ([secondary]; the spherical spiral method). Variants: charged particles, k-means clustering (GWKM: Somayehee, Nikkhah & Roshanian 2019, J. Navig. 72(4), 948, doi:10.1017/S0373463318001029, [abstract]).
    • Finding. Fibonacci lattices give near-equal-area cells for any point count, with at least 40% lower RMS error in area estimates than latitude–longitude grids. Spherical-spiral catalogues are the star-tracker standard for uniform onboard catalogues.
    • Verdict: keep (§5.2). cedar-solve's Fibonacci lattice fields for pattern generation plus HEALPix for star selection is a sound combination. No change.

    5.3 Rigorous epoch propagation¶

    • Citation. Butkevich & Lindegren, "Rigorous treatment of barycentric stellar motion", A&A 570, A62, 2014 (arXiv:1407.4664, [abstract]). The same formulae appear in the Gaia DR3 documentation.
    • Core idea. Closed-form propagation of all six astrometric parameters between epochs, reversible, with a full covariance transformation.
    • Verdict: ADOPT as the reference (§5.1). With UCAC4 (no parallax or radial velocity) the equations reduce to the zero-parallax case, which Fayde's file-backed catalogues use. Fayde grows each axis's position error with the proper-motion error, with no covariance transformation, and the result feeds the fit weights in §5.5.

    5.4 UCAC4 error budget and epoch policy¶

    • Citation. Zacharias et al., "The fourth US Naval Observatory CCD Astrograph Catalog (UCAC4)", AJ 145:44, 2013 (arXiv:1212.6182). [abstract]
    • Finding.
    • Formal position errors are 15–100 mas at the mean epoch, plus 10–20 mas systematic.
    • Proper-motion systematics are about 1–4 mas/yr; random errors are larger for faint stars.
    • Propagated to 2026 (about 26 yr from 2000, somewhat less from UCAC4's central epochs), typical position errors are therefore about 0.05–0.3″. At 3″/px that is 0.02–0.1 px: irrelevant for matching and small for the fit.
      • Gaia DR3 is about 100× better and would only matter for accuracy claims finer than about 0.1 px.
    • Verdict: ADOPT a two-level policy (§5.1, §5.2).
    • Build the index at a fixed epoch (for example 2025.0). For 99.9% or more of stars, |µ| < 100 mas/yr, so ±10 yr moves them less than 1″ (0.3 px), well within the pattern tolerance.
    • Exclude high proper-motion stars from the index (Fayde's cut is about 180 mas/yr per degree of field, §5.2). Query-time catalogue reads still see them.
    • Propagate at query time, to the mid-exposure epoch, for verification and fitting.

    Rebuilding the index for each campaign is then unnecessary. Gaia DR3 matters only for accuracy requirements finer than about 0.1 px.

    5.5 Aberration for spacecraft observers¶

    • Citations. Klioner, "A practical relativistic model for microarcsecond astrometry in space", AJ 125, 2003. [secondary] SOFA/ERFA iauAb/eraAb (fully relativistic to about 1 µas). Gravity Probe B context: orbital aberration about ±5.2″, annual about ±20.5″ (via search, [secondary]).
    • Finding (computed). For a telescope in low Earth orbit, β = |v_annual + v_orbital|/c is at most about 1.25×10⁻⁴. That shifts the field centre by up to about 26″. The differential effect across a 1° field is a scale change of order β, at most about 0.2″ at the field edge, about 0.07 px.
    • Verdict: CONSIDER ERFA's ab formulation (§6.6). At this FOV, aberration affects only CRVAL and the plate scale, so matching and verification can safely ignore it, and observer state would be an output-accuracy input, never a solve-rate input. Fayde takes no observer state and applies no aberration.

    5.6 Index compression¶

    • Finding. No result found that improves on cedar-solve's layout (a 16-bit key check plus the longest edge in each slot; Fayde stores the edge as f32) for Fayde's scale. Index size is covered in §5.2.

    6. Learned / ML star identification¶

    • Citations.
    • Hepburn-Dickins, Jones, Edwards, Morgan & Bell, "SIGNN – star identification using graph neural networks", WACV 2025 (CVF page, [abstract]). A graph-attention network over a Hipparcos star graph with edges below 3°.
    • Hepburn-Dickins & Edwards, "Noise robustness of data-driven star classification", ICPRAM 2023 (PDF, [full]).
    • RPNet (Xu et al. 2019), 1-D CNN classifiers (Yang et al. 2022), and Wang et al., Sensors 2021 ([secondary]).
    • Evidence.
    • ICPRAM 2023: 98.5% accuracy on augmented samples, but 13.5% of stars misidentified on simulated Stellarium images.
    • A 0.999 confidence cut removed 99.85% of wrong identifications but discarded 39% of correct ones.
    • No head-to-head comparison against Pyramid or tetra3.
    • Most learned methods are trained for wide-FOV Hipparcos star trackers. None addresses trailed stars, sub-degree FOVs with UCAC4 density, or calibrated false-accept rates.
    • Verdict: SKIP. Not credible for production where a wrong WCS is worse than no WCS. Fayde's correctness rests on a likelihood-ratio verifier with a hypothesis-count correction, which ML methods do not provide. If anything, a learned source classifier belongs on the producer side, feeding p_star, which the contract already supports.

    7.2 Centroid accuracy¶

    • Cramér–Rao bound, Gaussian star: √2·σ / SNR per axis background-limited (0.60 FWHM / SNR), σ / √N source-limited (King 1983 [full]; Mendez, Silva & Lobos 2013 [full]; Mortensen et al. 2010 [full], whose eq. 6 gives (16/9)·σ²/N + 8π·σ⁴·b/N² for a Gaussian-weighted centroid).
    • A matched filter followed by a 3 × 3 quadratic fit reaches the bound for FWHM 2.8 to 5.6 px at SNR 5 to 100; a fixed kernel does not at the extremes; a 7 × 7 centre of light is several times worse (Vakili & Hogg 2016 [full]).
    • A parabola through three samples of a Gaussian peak is biased towards the pixel centre; the same fit on the logarithms has no bias (Naidu & Fisher 1991 [full]).
    • Undersampled stars (σ 0.66 px): moment methods have pixel-phase floors of 0.02 to 0.04 px; only a pixel-integrated profile fit goes lower (Fialho 2025, arXiv:2507.17560 [full], preprint).

    Verdict: ADOPT the matched filter with a log-quadratic 3 × 3 fit; derive the error per source.

    7.3 Statistics of noise peaks¶

    • Height distribution and density of local maxima of smoothed Gaussian noise: Cheng & Schwartzman 2015, 2017, 2018 [full]; Bond & Efstathiou 1987 [full]. For a Gaussian kernel of σ = s, maxima above u per unit area tend to u·exp(−u²/2) / (2^{5/2}·π^{3/2}·s²), the figure LSST uses for false-peak counts (DMTN-006 [full]).
    • On pixels, with a kernel of about 1 px, the continuous formula is 25 to 35% conservative (Lin, Schwartzman & Davenport 2025, arXiv:2501.13239 [full], preprint).
    • False discovery rate for astronomical sources: Miller et al. 2001 [full], Hopkins et al. 2002 [full] (pixel-level); local rate: Efron 2008 [full]. No published use of the peak-height test for optical star detection was found (it is used on CMB and radio maps).

    Verdict: ADOPT the peak density as a threshold and null model, bounded by the pixel count.

    Recommended changes to the design doc (ranked)¶

    1. §5.3: add a minimal-sample hinted matcher in front of (or beside) the 4-star hash. When the scale (and parity) prior is tight:
    2. build a sorted pair list (k-vector) of the catalogue stars in the hint cone at query time;
    3. seed similarity hypotheses from image-star pairs, and from single stars when roll is known;
    4. verify each with the existing verifier.

    With half the sources false, a 2-star seed is all-true 4× as often as a 4-star pattern, and a 1-star seed 8× as often (§3.1). Pair or attitude voting (§1.3, §1.8) is the fallback. The 4-star hash stays the primary path for weak priors, and the quad kd-tree stays the blind path. Basis: Fischler & Bolles 1981; Mortari et al. 2004; Kolomenkin et al. 2008. 2. §5.3, §6.3: PROSAC-style ordering. Order sources for every matcher by a deterministic score built from p_star (after the §5.0 adjustments) and brightness, not by brightness alone. In SSA frames the brightest detections are disproportionately non-stars. Basis: Chum & Matas 2005. 3. §6.4, §6.3a: derive roll, scale and parity priors from the trail model. In target-tracking mode, the common star-trail vector together with the predicted apparent target rate (TLE plus observer) and the exposure time fixes roll (with sign, hence parity) and scale. Treat this as a derived prior with σ, recorded in the provenance with its origin. It turns many "hinted" solves into the 1-star-seed case of item 1. This is Fayde's own inference: the literature (Houtz & Frueh 2021) uses common trail geometry only as a filter. 4. §5.4: local optimization before acceptance, and an NFA report. Score hypotheses on a weighted refit (similarity → TAN, plus rate terms when there are endpoints), not on the raw seed transform. Count the hypotheses tested and report an NFA-style figure alongside the log-odds, optionally requiring NFA < ε in blind mode. Basis: Chum et al. 2003; a-contrario framework. 5. §5.5: one mixture likelihood shared by the verifier and the fitter. Replace "re-match within kσ" with IRLS over the same Gaussian-plus-distractor mixture the verifier uses, with per-source outlier priors from p_star. Fit and report a global error-scale factor s. Choose the SIP order by BIC or leave-one-out instead of an F-test. Basis: Hogg, Bovy & Lang 2010; MAGSAC (the idea only). 6. §5.5, §6.5, §9: persistent instrument distortion. Add fayde refit --calibrate, a joint fit over many solved frames for a persistent SIP stored in the InstrumentProfile. Per-frame fits then solve only the linear terms plus at most a low-order correction. This avoids overfitting SIP order 3 on 30–60 partly trailed stars per frame. Basis: Bernstein et al. 2017; SCAMP. 7. §5.1, §5.2, §11 question 2: epoch policy. Build the index at a fixed epoch and exclude high proper-motion stars from patterns. Propagate at query time with the Butkevich & Lindegren 2014 formulae, including covariance, for verification and fitting. UCAC4 errors at 2026 are 0.02–0.1 px at 3″/px, so Gaia DR3 is needed only for accuracy goals below about 0.1″. In §6.6, note that aberration (ERFA ab) affects only CRVAL and scale at 1° FOV, never solve rate. 8. §9: public benchmarks. Add the ESA Kelvins "Star Trackers: First Contact" scenes (10k scenes, up to 80 false objects each; a Tetra entry scored within 0.4% of the winner) as a public regression test for the matchers, after checking their terms. Follow Rijlaarsdam et al. 2020's evaluation considerations, and use LOST's Pyramid and voting implementations as independent baselines on the synthetic trailed-star fixtures.

    Explicitly not recommended:

    • learned star-ID;
    • QUEST/ESOQ (SVD plus weighted TAN is enough);
    • TPV or Zernike output;
    • MAGSAC as an algorithm;
    • grid and Liebe-triangle matchers;
    • non-dimensional star-ID;
    • rolling-shutter models in v1.
